The First Innings 2. Mira Sen had learned that a good innings was built from small choices: leave the ball, trust the footwork, and keep watching the field. The session demanded more than a clean stroke. A wet morning made the ball skid, so Mira shortened her backlift and trusted her balance. On the last ball of the net, she held her shape and sent a clean drive through the covers. Kabir Rao, waiting to bat, nodded once. The premise sounded simple: A talented young batter from a neighborhood club earns a place at a state cricket academy and must rebuild her game after a serious injury. Mira knew the difficult part was staying ready when nobody was watching.

The morning pitch was damp enough to punish a loose drive, and Mira spent the first hour learning which balls would stop in the surface. Mira marked her guard, checked the wind, and faced a faster bowler than the one listed on the schedule. Kabir stood at mid-off and called the length before the bowler released the ball, a habit that annoyed her until it saved a boundary. One edge died short of slip. Mira did not look at the coach; she marked the next ball and began again.

The afternoon session became a test of patience. Mira wanted to prove she belonged in the academy squad. Kabir wanted to know whether she could score without taking risks that left the lower order exposed. They argued beside the kit bags while rain gathered over the far boundary. Their coach reminded them that a team could not build a season around one player's highlights. They chose the next session carefully, knowing every small habit would show under pressure.

By evening, the trial match had tightened into a contest of singles. Mira stopped chasing the spectacular shot and began placing the ball into spaces the fielders had forgotten. Kabir settled at the other end, turning quick ones into twos and calling early enough to keep them safe. Their partnership was not pretty, but it changed the total and made the opposition captain move 3 fielders.

The innings changed the question. Mira was no longer asking whether she had enough talent to be selected. She was asking what the team would need from her when the pitch was slow, the score was behind, and her timing disappeared. She kept the worn ball from the final over in her kit bag. Kabir did not celebrate. He simply asked whether she would be at training tomorrow, and she gave him an answer that depended on the next morning's work.

At the end of the day, Mira closed the book, tightened the tape around her wrist, and walked back toward the nets before the lights went out. The final entry read: the scorebook records a decision that could change the season.
